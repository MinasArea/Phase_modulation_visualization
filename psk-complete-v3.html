What is BPSK?

BPSK is the simplest PSK. Only 2 positions on the circle — exactly opposite each other. The wave either stays normal (0°) or flips completely (180°).

The rule: Bit 1 → phase = 0° (wave normal). Bit 0 → phase = 180° (wave flipped). Every bit change flips the wave.

Why so reliable?

The 2 points are 180° apart — maximum possible distance. Noise has to push the signal more than 90° to cause an error. That almost never happens.

What is QPSK?

QPSK uses 4 phases at the 4 diagonal corners (45°, 135°, 225°, 315°). Each position carries 2 bits — called a dibit. Double the speed of BPSK with the same bandwidth.

Key insight: Instead of sending bits one at a time, QPSK groups 2 bits together and sends them as one symbol. 2 bits = 4 combinations (00, 01, 10, 11) = 4 phases. Perfect match!

Why diagonal positions?

Placing points at 45°, 135°, 225°, 315° (not at 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) gives equal distance from both axes. This makes it equally resistant to horizontal and vertical noise.

What is 8-PSK?

8-PSK places 8 points evenly around the circle, every 45°. Each symbol carries 3 bits (called a tribit). Three times more data than BPSK per symbol.

Analogy: BPSK = 2 bus stops. QPSK = 4 stops. 8-PSK = 8 stops. More stops means more destinations (data), but passengers (signals) can accidentally get off at the wrong stop (error) more easily.

The crowding problem

With 8 points, each is only 45° from its neighbor. Noise only needs to push 22.5° to cause a wrong reading — and that wrong reading means a 3-bit error.

What is 16-PSK?

16-PSK packs 16 points on the circle — one every 22.5°. Each symbol carries 4 bits. This is where pure phase modulation reaches its practical limit.

The problem: 16 points crammed on a circle means each point is only 22.5° from its neighbors. The slightest noise nudge (11.25°) sends the signal to the wrong point — causing a 4-bit error.

Why use QAM instead?

At 16 levels, it's smarter to also vary the amplitude (distance from center), not just the phase. This spreads the 16 points across 2D space instead of cramming them on a circle. 16-QAM beats 16-PSK in almost every scenario.

When is 16-PSK actually used?

Only when amplitude must stay constant — like certain satellite links that use nonlinear amplifiers. In those cases, changing amplitude causes distortion, so you're stuck with pure phase shifting.

The wall: 16-PSK is the practical ceiling of PSK. Modern 4G/5G systems switch to QAM-64, QAM-256 or higher — using both phase AND amplitude together for massive data rates.

The law: Every time you double the phases → +1 bit per symbol, but points get twice as close → noise danger doubles.

How 5G solves this: It uses Adaptive Modulation. Your phone constantly measures signal quality:
Weak signal → switch to BPSK (safe) · OK signal → QPSK · Strong signal → 16-QAM or 256-QAM (fast)
That's why your download speed changes depending on where you are!
